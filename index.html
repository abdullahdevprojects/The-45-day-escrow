The 45 day escrow: what happens in each stretch

Underwriting sets the length, not paperwork. Inside the 45 days sit four smaller clocks, and missing any of them changes who carries the cost.

Count from the date of contract acceptance. Most contingency deadlines use calendar days.

Closing dates in a property contract are counted in calendar days, and it is worth confirming the number rather than assuming. Work out the date 45 days from today against your contract.

On this page

    A 45 day escrow is the middle setting in a US home purchase. It is long enough for a lender to underwrite a mortgage and short enough that a seller will accept it. Miss one date inside it and the whole chain shifts, usually at the buyer's cost.

    Why closings land on 45 days

    The length is driven by underwriting, not by paperwork. A financed purchase needs an appraisal, a title search, a full underwriting review and a final loan approval, and those run in sequence rather than in parallel. Cash purchases close in 10 to 14 days because none of that applies.

    Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac loans typically underwrite in 2 to 4 weeks. Add the appraisal at the front and the three day disclosure rule at the end and 30 days becomes tight, 45 becomes comfortable, and 60 signals a complication such as a construction loan, a probate sale or a difficult title.

    What happens in each stretch

    Days 1 to 3: contract and escrow opened

    Signed contract goes to the escrow or title company, and earnest money is deposited. From this point every deadline in the contract counts from the date of acceptance, not from the offer date.

    Days 3 to 10: inspections

    The inspection contingency is usually 7 to 10 days. This is the cheapest exit in the whole process, and the only window where a buyer can walk away over condition and keep the deposit.

    Days 7 to 21: appraisal

    The lender orders it, the buyer pays for it, and the number matters more than the sale price. A low appraisal means either the seller drops the price, the buyer covers the gap in cash, or the deal renegotiates.

    Days 10 to 35: underwriting

    The quiet part where nothing looks like it is happening. Underwriters request documents in waves. Every day a buyer sits on a document request is a day added at the end.

    Days 30 to 40: title work and clear to close

    Title search, liens, easements, survey, and the payoff statement from the seller's existing lender. Title defects are the most common cause of a delay past 45 days.

    Days 42 to 45: disclosure, walkthrough, signing

    Under TRID rules the Closing Disclosure must be in the buyer's hands at least 3 business days before signing. Certain late changes restart that 3 day clock, which is why lenders resist last minute edits.

    The rule that catches buyers: do not open a credit account, change jobs or move large sums between accounts during underwriting. Lenders re-pull credit and re-verify employment shortly before closing, and a new car loan on day 38 can end the deal on day 43.

    The contingency dates inside the 45

    Loan approvalDay 21 to 30Deposit can be at risk if the loan fails after this
    Title reviewDay 30 to 35Objections to title become harder to raise

    Each of these is a separate clock running inside the main one. They are counted in calendar days from acceptance in most state contracts, though a few use business days, and the difference across a holiday week is real.

    What pushes a 45 day escrow past the date

    • Document lag. The single most common cause. Underwriting asks, the buyer takes four days, the file goes back in the queue.
    • Low appraisal. Renegotiation adds a week even when both sides agree quickly.
    • Title problems. An old lien, a boundary dispute, an unreleased mortgage from a prior owner, a missing heir on a probate sale.
    • Insurance. In flood, wildfire and hurricane zones, binding coverage has become the late stage blocker it never used to be.
    • Condo and HOA documents. Associations are slow, and the lender cannot clear to close without them.

    When the date slips, the fix is an extension addendum signed by both parties. Without it, the contract can technically be in default on the closing date, and rate locks expire, which turns a delay into a cost.

    Other 45 day deadlines

    The number is unusually common in finance and property. A 1031 exchange gives 45 days to identify replacement properties, followed by 180 days to complete the purchase, which is the same structure as this deadline running into a 180 day window. Many credit card issuers give 45 days notice before changing terms. US passport routine processing has run around 4 to 6 weeks, which lands near the same mark, and several insurers allow 45 days to file a claim.

    Compared with the short consumer clocks like 30 day guarantees, the 45 day deadlines in property come with real financial penalties rather than a lost refund, so they are worth diarising the day the contract is signed.

    Common questions

    Is 45 days a normal time to close on a house?

    For a financed purchase it is the common middle. Cash sales often close in 10 to 14 days, conventional mortgages in 30 to 45, and complicated files in 60 or more.

    Do the 45 days include weekends?

    In most state contracts the count is in calendar days from the date of acceptance, so weekends and holidays are included. A few contracts use business days for specific contingencies, so the wording matters.

    What happens if the closing does not happen on day 45?

    Both parties normally sign an extension addendum. Without one, the contract can be in default, the rate lock may expire and the seller may have the right to keep the earnest money depending on the contract terms.

    Can a buyer still back out at day 40?

    Usually only by forfeiting the deposit, because the inspection, appraisal and loan contingencies have expired by then. Those early contingency windows are where the low cost exits are.
